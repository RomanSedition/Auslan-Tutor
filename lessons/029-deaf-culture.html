Deaf culture & community

Auslan belongs to the Australian Deaf community. This lesson is mostly about the community itself, its history and values, and how to be a good guest in it as a hearing learner.

About the community

Deaf culture: Deaf with a capital D

Deaf (capital D) refers to people who identify as part of the Deaf community, with Auslan as their language and a shared culture. deaf (lowercase) can just mean not hearing. Many Deaf people see themselves as a cultural and linguistic minority rather than as having a disability, though they may still use disability-related supports and services.

Deaf culture: A short history of Auslan

Auslan developed from the sign languages brought to Australia from Britain and Ireland in the 1800s, especially British Sign Language. It grew in Deaf schools and Deaf clubs. For much of the 20th century, many schools used 'oralism' and discouraged or banned signing. Auslan survived anyway, through the Deaf community. Auslan has been recognised in Australian government language policy since 1987, and the name 'Auslan' was coined by Deaf linguist Trevor Johnston, who led the work behind the Auslan dictionary that became Signbank.

Deaf culture: Deaf clubs and events

State Deaf societies and Deaf clubs have long been the social heart of the community: sport, socials, church, information. Events like National Week of Deaf People (usually in September, around the International Day of Sign Languages) are good times for learners to get involved.

Deaf culture: Technology and access

Captions, video calls, text messaging, the National Relay Service, vibrating and flashing alerts, hearing aids and cochlear implants are all part of the picture. Attitudes to hearing aids and implants vary a lot in the community; it's a personal choice, and not something to comment on.

Deaf culture: Etiquette for hearing learners
  • Keep eye contact, and don't talk without signing when Deaf people are in the group.
  • Get attention with a wave or a tap; don't shout or grab.
  • Don't invent your own sign name; one may be given to you.
  • Don't act as an interpreter. Recommend a professional one.
  • Learn from Deaf teachers where you can, and go to community events.
  • Ask before filming anyone signing.

Put it together

Reflection

  1. Find your local Deaf society or club online, and find one upcoming event you could go to.
  2. Look up classes taught by Deaf teachers near you (TAFE, Deaf societies, community classes).
  3. Watch some Auslan content made by Deaf creators, and notice the role shift and depicting signs.
  4. Sign a short introduction you'd use at a Deaf event: name, that you're a learner, why you're learning.

Check yourself

Recap

Auslan is the language of the Deaf community. Learn from Deaf people, show up to community events, and be a respectful guest. That's how a learner becomes part of the conversation.
