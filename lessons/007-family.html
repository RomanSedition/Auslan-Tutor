Lesson 7People~30 min

Family

Talking about family is one of the first real conversations people have. You'll learn the core family signs, how some of them come from fingerspelling, and how to ask and answer “Do you have…?”

By the end you can…

  • Sign the main family members
  • Notice signs that come from fingerspelling (MOTHER, FATHER, SON)
  • Ask and answer YOU HAVE BROTHER? and HOW-MANY?
  • Briefly describe your own family

1 · Parents

Both hands circle out and come together: a group.

The letter M (three fingers) tapped on the palm.

The letter F tapped twice.

Starts at the forehead, then the fingers tap the other hand (MOTHER/FATHER style).
Signs made from letters

MOTHER is the fingerspelled M (three fingers on the palm) tapped, and FATHER is a tapped F. Your Lesson 1 fingerspelling pays off here. These are called initialised signs.

2 · Brothers, sisters, partners

Bent index finger rubs the side of the nose.

Two fists side by side rub against each other.

Touch the ring finger of the other hand, where a wedding ring goes.

HUSBAND / WIFE is about the wedding ring: the same sign covers husband, wife and spouse.

3 · Children and grandparents

Flat hand, palm down, pats at a child's height.

Fingerspelled S-O-N (often shortened to S-N).

A short two-handed sign in front of the chest. Watch at 0.5×.

Arms cradle and rock a baby.

Australia-wide: a two-handed sign in front of the chest. Southern: starts at the nose. Watch at 0.5×.

Starts with a movement, then the F of FATHER. Compare with FATHER.
SON is fingerspelled

Signbank shows SON as fingerspelled S-O-N, often shortened to just S-N. Short English words are often fingerspelled like this, and the spelling gets smoothed into almost a sign of its own.

4 · Have

Open hand closes into a fist, as if grasping.
Having, not having, how many
  • YOU HAVE SISTER?: eyebrows up (yes/no question).
  • ME HAVE SISTER TWO. Numbers often come after the noun, though you'll also see them before.
  • ME HAVE BROTHER + headshake = I don't have a brother.
  • BROTHER HOW-MANY?: eyebrows down.
Deaf culture: Deaf families

Most deaf children (commonly cited as around 90%) are born to hearing parents. A smaller number grow up in Deaf families with Auslan as their first language. Hearing children of Deaf parents are often called CODAs (Children of Deaf Adults) and many are native signers. Asking a Deaf person whether their family is Deaf or hearing is a common, friendly question.

Put it together

Mirror practice

Press M for the mirror and R to record a take, then compare it side by side with the reference.

  1. Initialised signs. Fingerspell M then sign MOTHER; F then FATHER. Feel the link.
  2. Family list. MOTHER, FATHER, SISTER, BROTHER, GRANDMOTHER, GRANDFATHER, without looking.
  3. Your family. Sign who's in your family, using HAVE and numbers: ME HAVE SISTER ONE, BROTHER TWO…
  4. Not having. Sign something you don't have with a headshake. Record it and check the headshake starts with the sentence.
  5. Questions. Ask: YOU HAVE BROTHER? (brows up) · SISTER HOW-MANY? (brows down).

Check yourself

Recap

MOTHER and FATHER come from the letters M and F; SON is fingerspelled. HAVE + headshake = don't have. Numbers often follow the noun: SISTER TWO.
